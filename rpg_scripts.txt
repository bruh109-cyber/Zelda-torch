Here's something about the ocean:

The deep ocean is one of the least explored places on Earth. More people have walked on the Moon than have visited the Mariana Trench — the deepest point on the planet, sitting nearly 11 kilometers below the surface. Down there, the pressure is about 1,000 times greater than at sea level, temperatures hover just above freezing, and no sunlight has ever reached the seafloor.
Yet life found a way. Creatures like the barreleye fish — with its transparent head and tubular eyes — drift through the midnight zone. Anglerfish lure prey with bioluminescent lanterns dangling from their foreheads. Giant isopods the size of footballs crawl along the sediment. Hydrothermal vents, discovered only in 1977, spew superheated water and support entire ecosystems that run not on sunlight, but on chemical energy — a process called chemosynthesis that rewrote our understanding of what life actually needs to survive.
We've mapped less than 25% of the ocean floor in high resolution. In a very real sense, we know more about the surface of Mars than we do about our own planet's depths. Whatever is still down there, waiting to be found, is almost certainly stranger than anything we've imagined.
